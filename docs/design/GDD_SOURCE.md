1. Game Overview
Scrap Kingdom: A Dead Country is a single-player scrapyard management and industrial business simulation set in a fictional country devastated by years of conflict.

The country is no longer at war, but its economy has not recovered. Factories have collapsed, supply chains have broken, transportation is unreliable, and many communities struggle to obtain ordinary machinery, tools, and replacement parts.

In this environment, discarded objects have become valuable resources.

The player begins with a small scrapyard beside a damaged road. With limited money, basic equipment, and a few opportunities, they must build a successful business by collecting, purchasing, sorting, repairing, dismantling, manufacturing, and selling goods.

What begins as a modest scrap business can eventually become a large industrial enterprise.

The game focuses on business decisions, resource management, production, discovery, workforce management, and expansion.

The central fantasy: Build an industrial empire by turning the remains of a broken country into something valuable.
1.1 Core design pillars
1. Resourcefulness: Almost every object has potential value.
2. Discovery: Experimentation reveals new manufacturing possibilities.
3. Business management: Every purchase, sale, employee, and investment matters.
4. Progression: The player gradually gains access to better facilities, workers, customers, and locations.
5. Visible development: The scrapyard should physically evolve as the business grows.
6. Meaningful choices: Selling an item immediately might be profitable, but processing it could create something much more valuable.
7. Accessible complexity: Deep systems should remain understandable and enjoyable rather than becoming tedious.
2. World and Setting
2.1 The country
The game takes place in a fictional country that has suffered years of war and industrial collapse.

The conflict has ended, but its consequences remain visible everywhere.
- Industrial factories have been abandoned.
- Warehouses contain forgotten machinery and supplies.
- Roads are damaged and transportation is unreliable.
- Small workshops have replaced some large manufacturers.
- Electricity and fuel are expensive or difficult to obtain.
- Communities depend on repairing old equipment.
- Independent traders have become important to local economies.
- Some regions are recovering while others remain almost abandoned.
The world should feel inhabited, not completely lifeless.

People continue working, trading, repairing buildings, transporting goods, and establishing small businesses.
2.2 Atmosphere
The visual atmosphere should be industrial, worn, and slightly melancholic.

The game should use:
- Rusted machinery.
- Abandoned industrial structures.
- Dusty roads.
- Damaged vehicles.
- Improvised workshops.
- Old warehouses.
- Scrap piles.
- Faded signs.
- Makeshift settlements.
The overall tone should balance hardship with opportunity. The player is building something useful in a damaged world.
2.3 Narrative approach
The game does not require a traditional linear campaign.

Instead, the world tells its story through:
- Customer conversations.
- Contracts.
- Location descriptions.
- Market shortages.
- Special opportunities.
- Abandoned facilities.
- Rare discoveries.
- Business relationships.
The player gradually learns about the country through ordinary business activities.
3. Core Gameplay Loop
The game revolves around a connected economic loop.
1. ACQUIRE

Purchase materials, trade, or scavenge locations


2. SORT AND STORE

Inspect, categorize, and organize incoming goods


3. PROCESS

Repair, dismantle, refine, or manufacture


4. SELL

Serve customers, fulfil orders, and complete contracts


5. REINVEST AND EXPAND

Improve facilities, recruit workers, and unlock opportunities

The cycle continues at a larger scale


Every stage should connect to the others.

For example:
- A scavenging expedition finds a damaged generator.
- The generator is brought to the scrapyard.
- The player decides whether to sell it, repair it, or dismantle it.
- Repairing it requires electrical components.
- Those components may need to be manufactured.
- Manufacturing requires a recipe, materials, and a suitable worker.
- The repaired generator can then be sold to a customer for a higher price.
This creates a chain of decisions rather than isolated activities.
4. Main Yard
The scrapyard is the player's primary location and the central visual element of the game.

It should evolve from a small, poorly organized property into a large industrial complex.
4.1 Yard layout
The yard contains:
- Entrance connected to an external road.
- Storage areas.
- Scrap piles.
- Workshops.
- Production facilities.
- Loading and unloading areas.
- Vehicle parking.
- Offices.
- Worker facilities.
- Expansion areas.
The road connection is especially important because it connects the business to the outside economy.

Customers, traders, delivery vehicles, and scavenging teams should arrive and depart through this entrance.
4.2 Camera and presentation
The main yard uses a 2D isometric perspective.

The camera should support:
- Zooming in and out.
- Moving around the property.
- Selecting buildings.
- Inspecting objects.
- Interacting with workers and vehicles.
- Touch-based navigation on mobile.
The player should be able to understand the layout without needing to constantly open management menus.
4.3 Building placement
Buildings should have defined footprints and placement requirements.

The player can place structures within the available property.

Construction should communicate:
- Required money.
- Required materials.
- Building footprint.
- Construction duration.
- Operational purpose.
- Capacity or production benefits.
Buildings should provide meaningful benefits rather than existing only for decoration.
4.4 Yard development
The yard gradually gains:
- More storage capacity.
- Larger workshops.
- Better machinery.
- More organized scrap piles.
- More workers.
- Additional vehicles.
- Specialized production areas.
Visual changes should clearly communicate business growth.

7. Inventory and Storage
Inventory management should be easy to understand but strategically important.

Players can:
- Search items.
- Filter by category.
- Sort by value.
- Sort by quantity.
- Inspect individual items.
- Move items between storage areas.
- Reserve materials for production.
- Reserve stock for customer orders.
- Sell selected quantities.
7.1 Storage capacity
Storage is limited by weight or volume.

Different goods consume different amounts of space.

For example:
- Small electronic components consume little space.
- Metal plates consume moderate space.
- Large engines consume substantial space.
When storage reaches capacity, the player must make decisions:
- Expand storage.
- Sell surplus materials.
- Process bulky goods.
- Delay new acquisitions.
- Build specialized storage facilities.
The game should never silently delete valuable inventory.
8. Trading and Economy
The player operates within a changing local economy.
8.1 Buying
The player can purchase:
- Raw materials.
- Spare parts.
- Tools.
- Equipment.
- Machinery.
- Specialized components.
Buying should involve decisions about quantity, price, storage capacity, and future demand.
8.2 Selling
Goods can be sold through:
- General market transactions.
- Customer orders.
- Direct business requests.
- Industrial contracts.
- Special buyers.
Selling immediately provides cash, but processing an item may increase its eventual value.
8.3 Dynamic market
Prices can change based on:
- Supply.
- Demand.
- Scarcity.
- Regional conditions.
- Market events.
- Item condition.
- Reputation.
- Business progression.
The player should be able to observe market trends.

A shortage of copper, for example, might increase the value of electrical components and encourage scavenging expeditions focused on electronics.
8.4 Business expenses
The player must manage:
- Worker wages.
- Fuel.
- Construction costs.
- Production expenses.
- Expedition costs.
- Vehicle maintenance.
- Facility operating costs.
- Equipment purchases.
Expenses should be visible before the player commits to a decision.
8.5 Financial recovery
Financial mistakes should create challenges without making the game permanently unwinnable.

Possible recovery methods include:
- Selling stored materials.
- Accepting smaller orders.
- Reducing unnecessary expenses.
- Dismantling unused machinery.
- Taking low-risk scavenging jobs.
- Pausing optional production.

12. Repair System
Repairing damaged items can produce higher-value goods.

The player sends an eligible item to a repair workshop.

14. Experimentation and Recipe Discovery
Recipe discovery is one of the game's defining systems.

The player should not begin with knowledge of every possible manufactured product.

Instead, experimentation gradually reveals new possibilities.
14.1 Experimentation
The player uses available materials and a workbench to test combinations.

The system should guide experimentation through compatible categories and experiment types without revealing every result.

Possible outcomes:
- New recipe discovered.
- Partial recipe clue.
- Successful experimental product.
- Material loss.
- Damaged input.
- Failed experiment with useful information.
Experimentation should encourage curiosity rather than random guessing.
14.2 Recipe notebook
All discoveries are recorded in a notebook.

Each entry includes:
- Recipe name.
- Description.
- Required materials.
- Required tools.
- Facility requirements.
- Expected output.
- Difficulty.
- Worker requirements.
- Mastery progress.
The notebook should support searching, sorting, and filtering.
14.3 Recipe mastery
Discovery does not immediately make a recipe suitable for reliable mass production.

Recipes have three difficulty levels:
Difficulty	Successful demonstrations required
Easy	1
Medium	2
Hard	3

Once mastered, the recipe becomes eligible for repeatable batch production.
14.4 Worker skill and failure
Workers can attempt recipes they know how to perform, but insufficient skill increases the possibility of failure.

Skilled workers provide:
- Higher reliability.
- Better production efficiency.
- Reduced material waste.
- Potentially improved output quality.
The player should be encouraged to develop specialist employees.

19. Events and Opportunities
Events create variety and provide unexpected business opportunities.

Possible events include:

- A trader offers rare electrical components.
- A nearby factory becomes accessible.
- A customer requests urgent machinery.
- A transport disruption increases travel costs.
- A damaged industrial vehicle becomes available.
- A large buyer offers a special contract.
Events should encourage decisions without creating unavoidable punishment.
20. Progression and Business Tiers
The player gradually develops the business through five major tiers.










Tier 1 — Small Yard

Basic storage, simple trading, small orders, introductory scavenging and basic recipes.













Tier 2 — Growing Business

More storage, specialist workers, improved processing and additional customers.













Tier 3 — Established Scrapyard

Multiple production jobs, larger contracts, capable vehicles and intermediate recipes.













Tier 4 — Industrial Operation

Advanced facilities, specialist staff and complex production chains.













Tier 5 — Industrial Empire

Large-scale production, valuable contracts, extensive logistics and premium opportunities.



Progression should depend on a combination of:
- Money.
- Reputation.
- Completed contracts.
- Facility development.
- Production capabilities.
- Recipe discoveries.
Money alone should not determine advancement.
21. Reputation System
Reputation represents how much customers and businesses trust the scrapyard.

It increases through:
- Successful orders.
- Timely deliveries.
- Quality products.
- Reliable contracts.
- Repeated customer relationships.
It decreases through:
- Missed deadlines.
- Failed commitments.
- Poor-quality deliveries.
- Abandoned contracts.
Reputation unlocks better business opportunities.

The system should allow recovery after mistakes.

Major systems:
System	Responsibility
Simulation	Time, jobs, economy and progression
Item data	Item definitions and properties
Recipe data	Inputs, outputs and requirements

Workers	Recruitment, skills and assignments

28. Explicit Scope Boundaries
The core game should not become an entirely different genre.

Avoid adding:
- Tactical combat.
- A massive open-world survival system.
- Multiplayer.
- Full rival-company simulations.
- Detailed worker life simulation.
- Full conversational AI for customers.
- Physics simulation for every object.
- Complicated repair minigames.
- Unrestricted crafting combinations.
- Excessive micromanagement.
- Complicated stock-market systems.
The focus remains scrapyard management and industrial business growth.

30. Final Design Philosophy
Scrap Kingdom: A Dead Country should be a game about transformation.

At the beginning, the player owns a tiny yard with limited equipment, very little money, and almost no influence.

They learn how to recognize valuable materials, find useful salvage, discover production methods, hire skilled people, and serve customers.

Eventually, the player is no longer simply collecting scrap.

They are operating a sophisticated industrial business that produces goods, supplies businesses, and supports the recovery of a damaged country.

The most important feeling should be:

"I started with a pile of junk. Now I run an empire."
